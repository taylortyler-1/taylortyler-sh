“I’ve got a great idea. Why don’t you build…”

Your customer is about to tell you the thing that's been on their mind. It solves some problem they've got and obviously you should just build it. I mean, a customer is giving you a pain point and a solution that you didn't even have to ask for.

“Of course we can do that. What a great idea,” came out of my mouth. The gears turned in my brain. We're going to sell more or reduce churn because of this idea a customer gave me. That's product right?

It wasn't long before I started writing the PRD, scoping the idea with engineering, and getting ready to humbly accept the promotion that was coming my way after this brilliant feature came out. 

I'm sure it really works out that way sometimes.

For me, that proposed idea ended up being the feature we needed once or twice. The other times, that great idea was just a symptom. A small manifestation of a disconnect between what we're offering and what the customer needs.

The trick is figuring out whether the customer is describing the problem or the workaround they've invented to deal with it.

## Don't Build the Symptom

When there's a gap between what a customer is trying to accomplish and what the product currently helps them accomplish, feature requests start to grow.

Customers know when something isn't working. They live with the problem every day. The first time they noticed the gap, they found a workaround. They had to.

By the time they're talking to you, that workaround has calcified. They've refined their workaround, complained to their coworkers about how the software is missing something, and started thinking of ways you could fix it.

That imagined fix is the feature request. The request arrives with the problem and the solution fused together.

We need a dashboard. An export. An alert.

It sounds like a complete diagnosis. The customer asked for X, so I need to build X. Problem solved. This product management stuff is easy.

Except that the solution they're proposing is shaped like the workaround they created. It works. It gets the job done. Now they want you to make it easier.

Occasionally that's exactly what you should do. Not every workaround points to a deeper problem.

Usually, it's a symptom of a larger issue. One that's showing up in multiple workarounds, support tickets, sales blockers, and an endless sea of feature requests across the product.

Fixing one of those symptoms will make that user's life easier. Do that enough times, and you can fill a roadmap with disjointed features and random stopgaps.

Now you've got a more complex product and the same exact problem.

## Three Feature Requests, One Problem

Let's make this a little more concrete.

Imagine you're working on a B2B software product that helps customers manage their accounts and workflows.

Over the course of a few weeks, you hear three requests.

**Customer A wants a dashboard.** They need a place to see which accounts are falling behind so they can figure out where to spend their time.

**Customer B wants a CSV export.** Every morning, they pull account information into a spreadsheet to figure out which accounts need attention.

**Customer C wants alerts.** They're tired of checking the platform throughout the day to see whether anything important has changed.

Three customers. Three perfectly reasonable feature requests.

You could build all three. Put them on the roadmap, write the tickets, and make everyone happy.

But take another look.

All three customers are doing extra work to answer a similar question: *What needs my attention right now?*

One wants a dashboard to answer it. Another has built a spreadsheet. The third wants the software to tell them.

The proposed features are different, but the gap might be the same.

Maybe the right solution is a dashboard. Maybe it's better notifications. Maybe the information already exists in your product and you've just made it too difficult to find.

You don't know yet.

But now you've got a problem worth investigating instead of three features waiting to be scoped.

That's a much better starting point.

## How Do You Find the Actual Problem?

Take a lot of lumps.

That's how I learned to tell the difference between a feature request and a festering big problem. Unfortunately, I spent a lot of time building a requested feature only to have the same problem still waiting for me.

Now that I'm a little wiser, I try to dig into what came before their amazing workaround.

I usually start with five questions:

1. **What were you trying to accomplish?**
2. **Walk me through the last time you tried.**
3. **What did you do when the product fell short?**
4. **Do you have any coworkers who run into this?**
5. **What happens if you can't solve the problem?**

These questions don't magically give you the big problem. They give you threads to pull on.

The first two help me understand what the customer is actually trying to do, without starting from their proposed feature.

The third tells me how they're getting around the problem today. Workarounds are interesting because they show what someone is willing to do to accomplish something your product isn't helping them do.

The fourth helps me understand whether this is a one-off frustration or something more people are dealing with.

And the fifth helps me understand the consequences. An annoying extra step and a problem that prevents someone from doing their job aren't necessarily worth the same investment.

Then I look across conversations.

Are different customers finding different ways around the same limitation? Are support tickets and feature requests pointing toward the same underlying issue?

As you pull those threads, that dashboard, export, and alert suddenly start to look similar.

The features are different. The gap is the same.

That's the big problem.

## So Should You Ignore Feature Requests?

No. Please don't.

Sometimes the customer has diagnosed the problem correctly and proposed a perfectly reasonable solution. You don't need to turn every button request into a six-week discovery initiative.

The point isn't to assume customers are wrong about what they need.

It's to avoid assuming the first solution you hear is the only one worth considering.

A feature request tells you something isn't working the way a customer needs it to. That's useful information.

But before you start building, spend a little time figuring out what they're really trying to accomplish.

You might discover that the feature they asked for is exactly what you should build.

Or you might find that solving the underlying problem makes three other feature requests disappear along with it.

Feature requests are still valuable.

**They tell you where to look.**
